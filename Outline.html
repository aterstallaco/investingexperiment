II

Things to keep in mind

Here are the two things you're buying — and why it might be fun to have the mix.

Stock
A share of one specific company. If you buy Ford (ticker symbol: F), you own a tiny piece of Ford Motor Company. Your money goes up or down based on how that one company does. Higher risk, but higher reward potential — you're making a personal bet.
ETF
An Exchange-Traded Fund — a bundle of many stocks packaged together, like a sampler platter. For example, an S&P 500 ETF holds pieces of the 500 largest U.S. companies. Instead of betting on one company, your money is spread across hundreds. Lower risk, steadier returns.
Why both?

Your stock pick is your gut instinct — the company you believe in wholeheartedly. Your ETF is a bit more of your safety net and includes a broader swath. Watching them side by side over a month should be fun to see.

III

How to Play

1

Use Your Brokerage Account

If you already have a brokerage account or IRA, you can buy directly from there. If you don't have one, most are free to open — just search for one that works for you. Look for one with no commissions and fractional share support for ETFs.

If you're playing along without real money, skip this step — just pick your stocks and ETFs and send them along and we'll get them added.

2

Find Your Picks

Browse Google Finance or Yahoo Finance to explore companies and ETFs.

For your stock pick: Think about companies whose products you use and believe in. Look at the share price — you need to afford at least 1 full share within your budget. A cheaper stock leaves more room for ETFs; an expensive stock is a bigger bet on one company. That tradeoff is part of the strategy.

For your ETF pick, here are some well-known options to explore:

  • S&P 500 ETFs — track the 500 largest U.S. companies (e.g., VOO, SPY, IVV)
  • Total Market ETFs — even broader, thousands of companies (e.g., VTI, ITOT)
  • Tech-focused ETFs — heavier on technology (e.g., QQQ, VGT)
  • Dividend ETFs — companies that pay regular dividends (e.g., SCHD, VYM)
  • International ETFs — companies outside the U.S. (e.g., VXUS, VT)

IV

How Do I Know What I Gained or Lost?

The simplest way: what did you pay, and what is it worth now?

Your brokerage account will show your current holdings and their market value. The difference between what you paid and what it's worth today is your gain or loss. The tracker calculates this automatically.

Don't worry about expense ratios or platform fees — on $100 over one month, those costs amount to fractions of a penny. What moves the needle is whether your stock and ETF prices went up or down.

V

A Few Things to Keep in Mind

You don't have to spend all $100 on one stock. A $15 stock leaves $85 for ETFs — and that diversification might outperform someone who went all-in on a single $90 stock.

Steady can win. Companies that make everyday products tend to be more stable. Exciting picks are fun but volatile.

Nobody knows the future. Even professional fund managers struggle to beat the market consistently. Don't stress about making the "perfect" pick — the point is to learn.

The real win is learning. After a month of watching real prices move, we'll understand a number of different areas better. That knowledge is worth far more than $100.

VI

Quick Glossary

Ticker
The short code for a stock or ETF. For example, AAPL = Apple, VOO = Vanguard S&P 500 ETF.
Share
One unit of ownership in a company or ETF.
Fractional Share
A piece of a share — like buying $12 worth of a $500 ETF. You own a small slice.
ETF
Exchange-Traded Fund — a bundle of stocks you can buy as one thing.
Portfolio
All of your investments together.
Return
How much your portfolio gained or lost, usually shown as a percentage.
Diversification
Spreading money across different investments to reduce risk.
